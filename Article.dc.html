The Quiet Return of the Town Hall

What Bengaluru's civic meetings reveal about public trust — and why the format is making a comeback.

AS
Ashwin Sundaresh
Sep 2, 2026 · 7 min read

A ward committee meeting in session, Bengaluru.

For a few years, the ward committee meeting looked like a relic. Attendance thinned, agendas ran long, and the loudest voice in the room usually won. Then, quietly, something shifted: more residents started showing up, and more of them started speaking.

It isn't nostalgia. It's necessity. As civic issues in Bengaluru have grown more local — a flooded underpass, a contested layout plan, a school that needs a crossing guard — the town hall has turned out to be the one format built for exactly this kind of problem: specific, geographic, and resolved through people who actually live with the outcome.

Why the room still matters

Social media can surface a grievance in minutes. It rarely resolves one. A civic complaint posted online travels fast and dies fast, buried under the next scroll. A town hall forces something a feed can't: two people who disagree, in the same room, unable to walk away without at least hearing each other out.

"The town hall doesn't move faster than the internet. It moves slower, on purpose — and that turns out to be exactly what civic trust needs."

This is also, we think, why formats like Echoes of Bengaluru have found an audience: an open mic is a town hall with better lighting. Strip away the agenda items and what's left is the same basic act — showing up, in person, to be heard by neighbors rather than followers.

None of this means the format is perfect. Meetings still run long. The same few residents still dominate. But the return of the room, however imperfect, is a signal worth paying attention to: people still want to solve problems with people, not just about them.
